# How Do Enterprises Identify Leaders Who Create Value?

*Executive Talent Intelligence · Season 1 · Introduction — independent research by Vaughn Berger*

What if a qualified executive and an enterprise recruiter are looking at the same opportunity, yet each sees a different set of signals? The candidate sees years of results, relationships and operational decisions. The recruiting team sees a role definition, an application, a limited review window and many competing priorities. Neither perspective is unreasonable. **The challenge is turning professional experience into evidence of future business value.**

That is the problem I intend to investigate—starting with my own executive job search.

## Abstract

Executive Talent Intelligence is a personally motivated, AI-assisted research project examining how major U.S. employers define, recognize and validate leadership talent. Rather than beginning with assumptions about résumés or applicant tracking systems, the inquiry starts with organizational needs and traces how those needs are translated into hiring decisions. This introduction presents current U.S. workforce data, explains the method and its limitations, and commits to reporting implementation results—even when they challenge the original premise.

**Keywords:** executive talent; enterprise recruiting; talent acquisition; applicant tracking systems; leadership evidence; skills-based hiring

## Why study hiring from both sides?

In April 2026, SHRM reported that **68% of 2,094 surveyed U.S. HR professionals** experienced difficulty recruiting for full-time positions. Among the respondents reporting recruiting difficulty, **53% said it had become harder** than one year earlier. These findings cover organizations across industries and sizes; they do not measure executive recruitment specifically. [SHRM, 2026](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-unveils-2026-talent-trends-report--data-driven-insights-for).

That distinction matters. A company's struggle to hire technical staff, frontline workers or senior executives may have different causes. A broad recruiting statistic cannot answer why a particular executive candidate was or was not invited to interview.

Yet the survey highlights a shared reality: employers have consequential staffing decisions to make, and experienced professionals need a credible way to communicate what they can contribute.

*The categories refer to distinct questions, may overlap, and are not executive-hiring estimates. The companion PDF contains the fully rendered, labeled chart.*

The accompanying figure shows three responses from the same SHRM survey: **68%** reported difficulty recruiting full-time workers, **42%** reported difficulty retaining them, and **41%** said they train existing employees for hard-to-fill roles. The categories are answers to different questions, not mutually exclusive groups. The data are unweighted and self-reported, so they describe participating HR professionals—not a representative census of U.S. employers. [SHRM, 2026](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-unveils-2026-talent-trends-report--data-driven-insights-for).

These figures suggest that hiring is part of a broader workforce problem. They do **not** show which executive qualifications predict performance, how a particular applicant system ranks candidates, or whether résumé changes improve interview rates.

SHRM's separate 2026 *Finding Talent* research surveyed **1,268 U.S. recruitment specialists** and examined skills-based hiring, recruiter–hiring manager partnership, and the use of technology in recruiting. Its published methodology includes December 17, 2025–January 26, 2026 field dates. It provides direction for further investigation, not proof of a universal executive-hiring workflow. [SHRM, 2026](https://www.shrm.org/about/press-room/shrm-unveils-finding-talent--rethinking-recruitment-for-today-s-).

## A different approach to the job search

My professional background spans enterprise business development, operations, and technology-enabled process improvement. In those roles, the most useful questions often came before the proposed solution: What outcome are we trying to achieve? What evidence would change our decision? Which assumptions have we not tested?

I am applying that discipline to an unfamiliar problem in a very personal setting.

The research will examine enterprise hiring economics, the actual decision steps between application and interview, documented ATS capabilities, leadership requirements, and a sample of current job requisitions. It will then compare those requirements with verifiable professional accomplishments and test different ways of presenting that evidence.

Crucially, the plan does not end with a better-looking résumé. It ends with **implementation, verified applications, measured first-interview outcomes and documented lessons**. A disappointing or inconclusive result will be reported as such.

## Three takeaways at the starting line

- **Hiring difficulty is not one-dimensional.** Recruiting, retention and internal development all appear in current workforce research; executive hiring needs its own evidence.
- **Systems should be investigated, not mythologized.** We will distinguish what a recruiting product can do from what an employer actually configures and uses.
- **Evidence must survive scrutiny.** A candidate's claims, an employer's requirements and the eventual application outcomes must all be traceable before we call the experiment successful.
